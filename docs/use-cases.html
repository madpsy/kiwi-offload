What a Kiwi running kiwi-offload can do

Practical set-ups, drawn out: what goes to a PC, what web listeners get, and which part of the Kiwi runs out first. Every diagram is the same picture of the board, so they can be compared.

What a stock KiwiSDR does

The KiwiSDR's own software (kiwid) is built for an open web SDR. The FPGA makes a fixed set of narrow receiver channels, and each listener, or each program such as kiwirecorder or wsprdaemon, takes one channel. The owner picks one of five FPGA modes on the admin page; they trade channels, audio bandwidth and waterfalls against each other.

  • Each channel carries audio or I/Q of up to 12 kHz (20 kHz in the 3-channel mode) to one user.
  • Each user has their own 0–30 MHz waterfall that zooms in. In Full 8 channel mode the eight users take turns on three waterfall DDCs: a user's waterfall locks a free DDC, captures one frame and releases it, and the FFT runs on the CPU afterwards. Sharing limits the zoom to level 11 (instead of 14), and the frame rate falls as more users compete, most at high zoom.
  • In More receivers mode only channels 0 and 1 have a waterfall; channels 2–7 show an FFT of their own audio, which covers only their passband.
  • The number of channels is the number of users at once, so the capacity is fixed and known, which is what a public listing needs.
  • A Kiwi registered on kiwisdr.com that ran Kiwi classic or More receivers is switched once to Full 8 channel, so almost every public Kiwi runs that mode.
  • All five modes ship for both KiwiSDR boards; the 14-channel mode is offered only on multi-core BeagleBones (BeagleBone AI, AI-64).

kiwi-offload uses the same board differently: 13 receivers of 52 kHz to 1.48 MHz each, shared by any number of listeners and a PC, within the board's limits.

From the KiwiSDR source (kiwid, September 2026): the modes in main.cpp, kiwi.config and the admin page; waterfall sharing in rx/rx_waterfall.cpp.

The same board with kiwi-offload

The rest of this page is about a KiwiSDR running kiwi-offload: what its parts are, what a PC or a web listener can ask for, and seven set-ups drawn out.

Decoders run in the listener's browser, not on the Beagle

kiwi-offload's FT8, FT4, FT2 and WSPR decoders are WebAssembly builds of jt9 and wsprd that run in each listener's own browser, as do the notch and noise reduction. They use the listener's computer, not the Beagle's one core, so however many listeners decode, the decoding itself adds nothing on the Beagle. It only demodulates the VFO as it does for anyone listening; a decoding VFO's audio is sent as 12 kS/s PCM, which costs about half a percent of the core more than ADPCM. The CPU figures on this page therefore hold whether or not listeners are decoding.

The three things that get shared

13 receivers (DDCs)

The FPGA has 13 receivers. Each one tunes a slice of the HF spectrum, from 52 kHz to 1.48 MHz wide. Only receivers 0–3 can go narrower than about 104 kHz, so very narrow bands (2200 m, 630 m, 60 m, 30 m) need one of those four. Receivers 9–12 have a second job: while one is not streaming, kbridge borrows it for the web listeners' zoomed waterfalls, and a listener or the PC can take it back at any moment.

Two links to the Beagle

Receivers send their samples over SPI (budget 40 Mbit/s) or the PRU lanes (45 Mbit/s), at most 87 Mbit/s together, the Ethernet's room. Wider slices and more bits per sample cost more. The full-band waterfall's snapshots use 2.8 Mbit/s of SPI; the zoomed waterfall's frames up to 7 more, at the lowest priority and outside the budget.

The Beagle's one CPU core

It forwards streams to a PC (kbridge: about half the core at 70 Mbit/s, two thirds at 85) and runs every web listener (about 4 % each with USB and ADPCM, plus the window they listen to). kweb keeps 15 % free and refuses what would not fit.

What each kind of client can ask for

A PC, with kiwi-offload's own protocol

Any usable width from 52 kHz to 1.48 MHz (the receiver's decimation R from 511 down to 18), centred anywhere from 0 to 33.3 MHz; 8, 10, 12, 14 or 16 bits per sample (12 by default); SPI, the lanes or either; a gain step from 0 to 8. Up to 13 slices, one per receiver.

A PC, as HPSDR software

Through kiwi-hpsdr-bridge: 48, 96, 192 or 384 kHz per receiver, up to 10 receivers (HPSDR protocol 2) or 1 (protocol 1), delivered as 24-bit samples. The bridge's --bits (8–16) sets what crosses the Kiwi's links.

A web listener

The free-tuning window and own receivers: 52, 104, 208, 306 or 417 kHz wide (up to max_width); band windows: as wide as the configuration sets. Up to 4 VFOs each, each a ±12 kHz audio channel at 24 kS/s, in USB, LSB, CW, AM, narrow AM, SAM (also its upper, lower and stereo forms), NFM or IQ; audio as ADPCM (96 kbit/s, the default), PCM at 12 kS/s, Opus (32 kbit/s) or Opus HQ (48 kbit/s).

The waterfalls

The full band

Besides the 13 receivers, the FPGA sends short raw snapshots of the whole band, 0–33.3 MHz: 16 384 ADC samples, about 246 µs of signal each. kbridge turns each snapshot into one waterfall row of 8192 points, 4.07 kHz apart. It needs no receiver, every web listener sees it, and a PC can have the rows too. It is on in every set-up below except the widest view, which moves the snapshots to the lanes: kbridge builds the waterfall only from snapshots that arrive on SPI rules.

Set in [kbridge]:

  • snapshots = 100: a snapshot every 100 ms, 10 rows a second, 2.8 Mbit/s of SPI measured. Each snapshot is 280 kbit, so 200 ms halves the rows and the link cost and 50 ms doubles them rules.
  • snapshots = 100:L: the snapshots go over the lanes, which frees SPI but leaves no full-band waterfall rules.
  • No snapshots line: no snapshots, no full-band waterfall. (snapshots = 0 does not turn them off: kbridge reads anything under 1 ms as 100.) rules

A snapshot covers about 246 µs every 100 ms, so the waterfall samples the band rather than watching it; the receivers' own waterfalls and the audio see everything.

Zoomed in: dual-purpose receivers

With [kweb] zoom_waterfall = on, as the example configuration ships, each listener has one waterfall that zooms from all of 0–33.3 MHz down to 2 kHz across. Zoomed in, the detail comes from a receiver that is already running there (that window's own rows, or finer FFTs of its samples), and elsewhere from DDC snapshots: kbridge retunes a free receiver of 9–12 to each listener's view in turn and captures one frame of 1024 points across the view. A listener or the PC that wants that receiver takes it at once, and the snapshots move to the next free one; with all four streaming, zoomed views fall back to the full band's 4 kHz rows rules.

  • Up to 10 frames a second per view (zoom_fps) and 100 in all; 20 in all while the slices on SPI take more than 33 Mbit/s rules.
  • A frame is 70 kbit on SPI, at the lowest priority and outside the link budget: 7 Mbit/s at 100 frames a second. It needs the full-band snapshots on SPI too, so snapshots = 100:L turns it off rules.
  • One receiver serves every view in turn: ten listeners zoomed in at once, each somewhere different, got 8.3 rows a second each. Listeners looking at the same stretch of band share frames measured.
  • It is worth having only with CPU to spare (kbridge about 0.26 % per frame a second), so kweb lowers the frame rate whenever its CPU guard is short, and to 2 a second at once for a listener who would not fit otherwise measured.
  • zoom_waterfall = off gives the classic layout instead: the full band plus the narrow waterfall of the window the listener is in, 20 rows a second.

Each listener can turn it off

How it is sent

Each point is one byte, 0.5 dB per step. kweb shrinks every row to the listener's own screen width (keeping the strongest point under each pixel) and then sends either the whole row or only what changed: a 1-bit-per-pixel mask plus one byte for each pixel that moved by 3 dB or more (min_diff_db). A whole row goes every 50 rows, after a zoom or pan, and whenever the change list would not be smaller. The full band comes one row per snapshot, 10 a second; the classic layout's narrow waterfall 20 a second. A PC gets kbridge's full 8192-point rows in its stream, coded the same way.
